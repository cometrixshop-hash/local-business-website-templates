How to Write Website Copy That Gets Calls: A Plain-English Guide

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Visitors decide in seconds whether to call you or leave. Most of that decision is driven by words: whether the page answers "do they fix my problem, do they come to me, and can I trust them?" This guide is a plain-English process for writing website copy that gets calls, without hype, invented claims or paragraphs nobody reads.

Write for the person with the broken thing

Your customer is not browsing for inspiration. A homeowner with a flooded basement, a salon client who needs Saturday hours, a contractor looking for a roofer for a real estate closing — each arrives with a problem and a phone in hand. Write to that person.

The fastest way to find the right words is to listen to your own office. Write down the ten questions your staff answers most often. Those questions, in the customer's own words, are your headings and your FAQ. If your front desk hears "Do you come to [neighborhood]?" every day, that belongs in your headline or your service area section, not buried on page three.

The structure that works

You do not need a novel. A one-page site with these blocks, in this order, outperforms most five-page sites:

  1. A headline that names the problem and the place. "Water heater repair in Mesa, AZ — same-day service." Not "Your comfort is our priority."
  2. A one-line promise and proof. "Licensed and insured · 200+ local reviews · 90-day workmanship warranty." Only list proof you can back up.
  3. Services, organized the way customers search. Group by problem ("No hot water", "Leaking tank", "New installation"), not by internal job codes.
  4. What happens next. Three short steps: call or request, diagnosis and upfront price, fix and follow-up. This removes the anxiety that stops people calling.
  5. Trust signals. Real photos of your team and work, license number, genuine reviews, years in business, owner's name.
  6. Service area. A readable list of cities or ZIP codes. Match your Google Business Profile exactly.
  7. FAQ. Answer objections: pricing, timing, brands, financing, guarantees, parking, pets, etc.
  8. A clear contact block. Phone, form, hours, and a line for urgent jobs.

Six rules that make copy convert

1. Specific beats superlative

"We answer live, 7am–7pm" is useful. "Best plumber in town" is noise. "Same-day water heater replacement" tells the visitor something they can act on.

2. Lead with the outcome, not the feature

Feature: "We use 50-gallon power-vented units." Outcome: "Hot water again today, with a 6-year tank warranty." Write the outcome first; put the technical detail in the FAQ.

3. Use the customer's words

If they say "my AC is blowing warm air," write "AC blowing warm air?" somewhere on the page. It signals that you understand, and it matches what they typed into Google.

4. Keep sentences short

One idea per sentence. Short paragraphs of two to three lines. Bullets for anything list-like. Aim for language an average adult can read quickly — plain language is also better for accessibility and for screen readers.

5. One primary action

Every page should have one obvious next step, and for most service businesses that is a phone call. Make the number a tel: link in the header, hero and footer, and on mobile put it in a sticky bar that stays visible while scrolling:

<a class="call-btn" href="tel:+155****0147">Call (555) 555-0147</a>

A contact form is a useful second option for non-urgent jobs, but it should never be the only way to reach you. Add a line above it: "No hot water right now? Call us — it's faster."

6. Do not invent

No fake reviews, no invented "since 1985" dates, no "award-winning" claims you cannot substantiate, no stock photos presented as your own team. Beyond being dishonest, fabricated claims are a common source of customer disputes and refund demands. If you have been in business three years, say three years — it is not a weakness.

Search basics while you're at it

Good copy and basic search setup reinforce each other. Google's SEO starter guide is the authoritative reference; the short version:

  • Title tag: one per page, with the main service and city. "Water Heater Repair in Mesa, AZ | Desert Air Plumbing."
  • Meta description: one or two sentences, written like a short ad, with a reason to click.
  • One H1 matching the page's topic; H2s for each section above.
  • Structured data: use the most specific LocalBusiness subtype for your trade, and keep name, phone, address and hours identical to your Google Business Profile. Google's LocalBusiness documentation lists the supported properties.
  • Mobile: Google uses the mobile version of your content for indexing, so check every page on a real phone over mobile data.

Editing pass: read it out loud

Once you have a draft, read it out loud. Anywhere you stumble, rewrite. Then ask three questions:

  1. Can a stranger tell, in five seconds, what you do and where?
  2. Is there one obvious number or button to tap?
  3. Would you be comfortable if the customer quoted your copy back to you in a dispute?
