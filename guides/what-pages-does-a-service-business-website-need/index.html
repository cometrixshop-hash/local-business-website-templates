What Pages Does a Service Business Website Need? A Simple Sitemap

Published 2026-11-08 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Most service business websites do not need many pages. They need the right pages: the ones a customer checks before calling, and the ones a search engine reads to work out what you do and where. This guide gives you a simple sitemap you can copy, plus the rules for deciding what to include and what to leave out.

Plan around the customer's questions, not your org chart

A visitor to a plumber's site is usually asking three things: do you fix my problem, do you work in my area, and how do I reach you right now. Every page you add should answer one of those questions, or help the visitor take the next step. If a page does neither, it is probably not earning its place.

The five pages that almost always earn their place

PageWhat it needs to do
HomeSay what you do, where you work, and how to call — in the first screen. Add proof (reviews, licenses, years in business) and one clear action.
ServicesList what you do, grouped the way customers search. A short intro, the services, and a call to action on each group.
Service areaName the towns and postcodes you cover. This is also where search engines confirm you serve the location.
AboutWho you are, how long you have been around, and why a stranger should trust you with their home or business.
ContactPhone, email, a form, and your hours. Make the phone number a tap-to-call link on mobile.

Service pages: one per service, or one grouped page?

Both can work; the choice depends on how different your services are and how much you have to say about each.

  • One grouped services page — best when your services are similar and short to explain (a cleaner's regular, deep and move-out cleans). It is simpler to build and keep current.
  • Separate pages per service — worth it when each service needs its own detail, pricing, photos or FAQ (a roofer's repair versus replacement versus gutter work). Separate pages also give you a page to target for each search term.

A good middle ground: one services overview page that links to a dedicated page for each major service. That gives you the depth without a maze of thin pages.

Location pages: when they help and when they hurt

If you serve several distinct towns, a short page for each can help you appear in local searches. But a page that is mostly a rewrite of the same text with a town name swapped in is a problem: it is thin, it can look like spam to search engines, and it gives visitors nothing new.

Write a location page only if you can say something specific: recent jobs in that town, a local landmark or route, the areas you cover, and real travel times. If you cannot, list the towns on your service area page instead.

Other pages worth having

  • Reviews and testimonials — with permission, and only real ones.
  • FAQ — answers the questions that come up on every call. This also reduces the number of enquiries you have to answer.
  • Financing or pricing — if you offer payment plans or published prices, a clear page helps.
  • Careers — only if you are actually hiring.
  • Privacy policy and terms — usually needed if you collect enquiries through a form or run ads; check what applies to you.

Pages you can safely skip

  • News or press pages, unless you genuinely have news.
  • Case studies for a one-person business with no real examples.
  • Testimonials you wrote yourself.
  • A separate page for every service you could ever offer — thin pages hurt more than they help.
  • Long "about the owner" essays — a paragraph or two is enough.

A simple sitemap you can copy

Home
├── Services
│   ├── Service A
│   ├── Service B
│   └── Service C
├── Service area
├── Reviews
├── FAQ
├── About
└── Contact

For a single-service business, collapse that to Home, Service, Service area, About, Contact. Fewer pages, each one better, beats a bigger site with nothing to say.

Navigation rules

  • Keep the main menu to five to seven items.
  • Put the phone number in the header on every page, and make it tap-to-call on mobile.
  • Give every page one obvious next step (call, book, or request a quote).
  • Use plain words for menu labels — "Services", "Areas", "Contact" — not clever ones.
  • Make sure a visitor can reach any page within a couple of taps.
