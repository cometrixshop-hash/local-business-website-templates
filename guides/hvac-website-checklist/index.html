What Every HVAC Website Needs to Get More Calls: A 12-Point Checklist

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

An HVAC company's website has one main job: turn a worried homeowner into a phone call. When the furnace quits on a cold night or the AC dies during a heat wave, people don't browse. They scan a page for a few seconds, look for signs that you're local, legitimate and available, and then either call or hit the back button. This checklist covers the elements that make the difference, in roughly the order a visitor sees them.

1. A phone number you can't miss

The phone number belongs in the header, in the hero section and in the footer, and on mobile it should also live in a sticky "Call now" bar that stays on screen as the visitor scrolls. Make it a real tel: link so a tap opens the dialer:

<a class="call-btn" href="tel:+15555550147">Call (555) 555-0147</a>

Test it on an actual phone. A number that's an image, or text that can't be tapped, is a surprisingly common reason sites lose calls.

If you take calls after hours, say so right next to the number ("Answering 24/7 for no-heat and no-cool emergencies"). If you don't, show your hours. Either way, set the expectation before they dial.

2. A headline that names the problem and the place

The first screen should answer two questions: "Do they fix my problem?" and "Do they come to me?" Something like:

Heating & air conditioning repair in Mesa and the East Valley. Same-day service on most calls.

Avoid generic lines like "Your comfort is our priority." They're true of every HVAC company and tell the visitor nothing. Only claim "same-day" if you deliver it most of the time.

3. Services split by what customers search for

Homeowners think in problems, not in equipment categories. Organize services the way they'd describe them:

  • AC repair and AC not cooling
  • Furnace and heating repair
  • Heat pump service
  • New system installation and replacement
  • Seasonal maintenance and tune-ups
  • Ductwork, thermostats and indoor air quality

Give each a short card with two or three sentences in plain language. If one service brings in most of your revenue, such as system replacements, consider giving it its own page later, with details on brands you install, financing if you offer it, and what the install day looks like.

4. Proof that you're real and qualified

An HVAC visit means letting a stranger into your home and often making an expensive decision. Trust signals carry a lot of weight:

  • License and insurance. If your state or city licenses HVAC contractors, show your license number. Visitors can look it up, and that transparency itself builds trust.
  • Real photos. Your trucks, your technicians, your completed installs. Stock photos of smiling models in spotless uniforms are easy to spot.
  • Reviews from real customers. A handful of genuine reviews with first names and towns. Link to your Google Business Profile so people can read more. Don't invent or "edit" reviews.
  • Manufacturer or industry credentials, only if you actually hold them.
  • Years in business and a short "about us" with the owner's name. People like to know who they're hiring.

5. A clear service area

List the cities, neighborhoods or ZIP codes you cover. This does two things. It stops out-of-area calls you'd have to turn down, and it gives search engines clear signals about where you operate. Keep it as a simple readable list or a short paragraph; long blocks of repeated city names look spammy to people and search engines alike.

Your service area on the website should match the one on your Google Business Profile. Google's service area help page explains how to set up to 20 service areas by city, postal code or region, and notes that if you don't serve customers at your address, you should remove it from the profile. Our local SEO checklist goes deeper on keeping these details consistent.

6. "What happens when I call?"

A short three-step section reduces anxiety:

  1. Call or request service. Tell us what's going on; we'll give you an arrival window.
  2. Diagnosis and upfront price. The technician explains the problem and the cost before starting work.
  3. Fix and follow-up. We clean up, test the system, and explain what we did.

Adjust to match how you really work. If you charge a diagnostic fee, say what it is and whether it's credited toward the repair. Being upfront about fees heads off a common source of bad reviews.

7. An FAQ that handles objections

Answer the questions your office already hears every week:

  • Do you charge for estimates on new systems?
  • How soon can you come out?
  • Repair or replace: how do I decide?
  • Do you offer maintenance plans?
  • Which brands do you service?
  • What payment and financing options do you have?

An FAQ saves phone time and captures the specific, long-tail questions people type into search engines.

8. A short contact form for non-urgent jobs

Not everyone wants to call. Someone planning a replacement or booking a tune-up may prefer to send a request at 11 pm. Keep the form to name, phone, address or ZIP, type of service, and a message. Add a note above it: "No heat or no AC? Call us. It's faster."

On a static site, services like Formspree or Netlify Forms can receive the submissions and email them to you. See How to add a contact form to a static site for setup steps and spam protection.

9. Speed and mobile layout

Google uses the mobile version of a site's content for indexing and ranking, and your visitors are often standing next to a broken thermostat with a phone in hand. Practical steps:

  • Resize photos before uploading. A hero image doesn't need to be wider than about 1600px.
  • Use loading="lazy" on images below the first screen.
  • Avoid heavy sliders, autoplay video backgrounds and pop-ups that cover the phone number.
  • Check the site on a real phone over mobile data, not just on your office computer.

A plain HTML and CSS site is usually very fast because there's no database or heavy framework behind it.

10. HTTPS with no warnings

If your site isn't served over HTTPS, browsers will flag it. As of October 2026, Chrome asks users for permission before making an insecure HTTP connection by default. For a business asking people to trust it with their home, that's a serious handicap. Most modern hosts include free certificates; the fix is covered in Why a 'Not secure' warning costs you customers.

11. Search basics that are easy to get right

  • Title tag: include your main service and city, e.g. "AC & Furnace Repair in Mesa, AZ | Desert Air HVAC."
  • Meta description: one or two sentences that read like a short ad, with a reason to click.
  • Headings: one H1 for the page's main topic; H2s for each section.
  • Structured data: schema.org has a specific HVACBusiness type. Include your name, phone, address or service area, and hours, matching your Business Profile exactly.
  • Google Business Profile: link your website from it, and link to it from your website.

12. Seasonal updates

HVAC demand swings with the weather. Before summer, move AC repair and tune-ups to the top of the services list; before winter, move heating. Update any seasonal promotions and remove expired ones promptly. An expired offer makes a site look abandoned.

Quick audit: score your current site

Open your site on a phone and check each item:

  • Phone number tappable in the first screen and in a sticky bar
  • Headline names your services and area
  • Services organized by customer problems
  • License, real photos and genuine reviews visible
  • Service area listed and matches your Business Profile
  • Process and fees explained
  • FAQ answers common objections
  • Working contact form, tested
  • Loads quickly on mobile data
  • HTTPS with no browser warnings
  • Unique title, meta description and HVACBusiness structured data
