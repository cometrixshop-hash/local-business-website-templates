Local SEO Checklist for Small Service Businesses: Google Business Profile, NAP and Schema

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Local SEO for a service business comes down to a simple idea: when someone nearby searches for what you do, Google should have clear, consistent, trustworthy information about you. You don't need tricks for that. You need a complete Google Business Profile, business details that match everywhere they appear, a website that clearly describes your services and area, and a steady stream of genuine reviews. This checklist walks through each piece in the order that tends to matter most.

How Google decides local rankings

Google's own help page on local ranking says local results are mainly based on three factors: relevance (how well your profile matches the search), distance (how far you are from the searcher or the location in the search), and popularity, which reflects how well known your business is. The same page states plainly that there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.

You can't change where a searcher is standing. You can influence relevance (by describing your business completely and accurately) and popularity (through reviews, a real web presence, and being mentioned by others). Everything below targets one of those two.

Part 1: Google Business Profile

For most local service businesses, the Business Profile is the single most important listing. It's what appears in the map results and in the panel on the right side of search results.

Claim and verify

  • Search for your business on Google Maps. If a profile exists, claim it; if not, create one at business.google.com.
  • Complete verification. Google's local ranking page notes that verification tells Google you're authorized to represent the business, making it more likely to show up in results.
  • Have only one profile per business. Google's guidelines for representing your business say duplicates can cause problems with how your information displays.

Get the basics exactly right

  • Name: use your real-world business name, as it appears on your signage, vehicles and paperwork. The guidelines say your name should reflect your business's real-world name, and they frame following the guidelines as the way to avoid suspension. Stuffing keywords or cities into the name ("Acme Plumbing – Best Emergency Plumber Springfield") breaks that rule.
  • Category: choose the most specific primary category (for example "Plumber" rather than "Contractor"). The guidelines advise using the fewest categories it takes to describe your core business.
  • Address or service area: if customers come to you, show your address. If you go to customers and don't serve them at your address (typical for plumbers, cleaners and HVAC), Google's service area guidance says to remove the address and set a service area instead. You can list up to 20 service areas by city, postal code or region.
  • Hours: keep regular and holiday hours current.
  • Phone and website: use your main business number and your own website URL.

Fill in the rest

  • Business description: describe what you do, who you serve and your history. Google's guidelines don't allow links or promotional pricing in this field.
  • Services: list each service you offer, with short descriptions.
  • Photos: real photos of your team, vehicles, completed work and (if applicable) your storefront. Google's local ranking page recommends adding photos and videos.
  • Attributes: add any that apply and are accurate.

Part 2: NAP consistency

NAP stands for Name, Address, Phone number. The idea is simple: every place your business appears online should list these the same way. When your website says "Acme Plumbing LLC, 12 Oak St, (555) 555-0123" and an old directory listing says "Acme Plumbing & Heating, 412 Main Street, (555) 555-0987," it's harder for people and search engines to be sure which details are correct.

Google's local ranking page says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local results. Consistent NAP is how you keep information accurate across the web.

How to audit your NAP

  1. Pick a canonical version. Decide exactly how you'll write your name, address (including "St" vs "Street", suite numbers) and phone number. Write it down and use it everywhere.
  2. Search for yourself. Search your business name, your phone number (in quotes) and old phone numbers or addresses. Note every listing you find.
  3. Fix the major listings first. Your Google Business Profile, your website, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, Facebook, Yelp, and any industry directories you use, plus your local chamber of commerce or trade association if you're a member.
  4. Close or update old listings. If you've moved or changed numbers, update old listings rather than creating new ones beside them.
  5. Keep a list. A simple spreadsheet of every listing, its login and the date you last checked it makes future changes much easier.

Common NAP mistakes

  • Using a call-tracking number on some listings and your main number on others, without a plan.
  • Different business names on different platforms (legal name on one, trade name on another).
  • A home address showing on a directory when you're a service-area business that hides your address on Google.
  • Leaving an old address on your website footer after a move.

Part 3: Your website

Your website gives Google, and customers, the detail that a Business Profile can't hold.

On-page basics

  • Title tag with your main service and city: "Drain Cleaning & Plumbing Repair in Springfield, IL | Acme Plumbing."
  • A clear H1 that says what you do and where.
  • A services section (or a page per main service) written in the words customers use.
  • A service area section that matches your Business Profile.
  • NAP in the footer, written exactly as your canonical version, with the phone as a tap-to-call link.
  • Mobile-friendly layout. Google uses the mobile version of your content for indexing and ranking.
  • HTTPS. Google announced HTTPS as a ranking signal back in 2014, describing it at the time as a very lightweight one. More importantly, browsers now warn visitors away from HTTP sites; see our guide to the 'Not secure' warning.

LocalBusiness structured data

Structured data is a block of code that describes your business in a standard vocabulary. Google's Local Business structured data documentation explains how it can tell Google about things like your business hours and departments. Use the most specific type from schema.org/LocalBusiness that fits: Plumber, HVACBusiness, Electrician, RoofingContractor, AutoRepair, HairSalon and so on.

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Electrician",
  "name": "Brightline Electric",
  "url": "https://www.brightline.example/",
  "telephone": "+1-555-555-0199",
  "image": "https://www.brightline.example/img/van.jpg",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "streetAddress": "88 Elm St",
    "addressLocality": "Dayton",
    "addressRegion": "OH",
    "postalCode": "45402",
    "addressCountry": "US"
  },
  "areaServed": ["Dayton", "Kettering", "Beavercreek"],
  "openingHoursSpecification": [{
    "@type": "OpeningHoursSpecification",
    "dayOfWeek": ["Monday","Tuesday","Wednesday","Thursday","Friday"],
    "opens": "08:00",
    "closes": "17:00"
  }]
}
</script>

Rules of thumb:

  • The name, address and phone must match what's visible on the page and your Business Profile.
  • Don't add review markup for reviews of your own business. Google's review snippet documentation supports LocalBusiness reviews only for sites that capture reviews about other local businesses, and warns against self-serving reviews.
  • Validate with Google's Rich Results Test after you publish.

Help Google find your pages

  • Create a free Google Search Console account and verify your site.
  • Submit an XML sitemap. Google's sitemap documentation recommends using fully qualified, absolute URLs in it.
  • Use Search Console's URL Inspection tool on your home page to confirm Google can see it.

Part 4: Reviews

  • Ask every happy customer. A short text or email after the job with a direct link to your review form works better than a sign on the wall.
  • Reply to reviews, good and bad. Google's local ranking page notes that replying shows you value feedback. Keep replies to negative reviews calm and factual, and take the details offline.
  • Never buy, fake or incentivize reviews. Google's prohibited content policy bans offering payment, discounts or free services in exchange for reviews, and says incentivized reviews will be removed from Maps.

Part 5: Local mentions and links

Being mentioned by real local organizations supports the "popularity" factor. Good, legitimate sources include your chamber of commerce, trade associations, suppliers' "find an installer" directories, local news coverage, sponsorships of local teams or events, and partners you work with (a plumber and a remodeler linking to each other, for example). Avoid paid link schemes.

The monthly 20-minute routine

  1. Check your Business Profile for suggested edits from Google or the public, and correct anything wrong.
  2. Reply to new reviews.
  3. Add a few new photos of recent work.
  4. Update hours for upcoming holidays.
  5. Glance at Search Console for errors.
