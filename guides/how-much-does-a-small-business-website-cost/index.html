How Much Does a Small Business Website Actually Cost?

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

There is no single answer to "how much does a website cost", because the price depends almost entirely on three choices: who builds it, where it lives, and how you maintain it. This guide gives you an honest breakdown of each, so you can compare quotes and options without guesswork.

The three cost buckets

Every website, from a one-page brochure to a large custom build, has the same three costs. The difference is how much each one costs.

1. The domain name

Usually the smallest line item. A .com typically renews in the $10–$20 per year range, and some registrars include privacy protection for free. Some TLDs are much more expensive, and some promotional first-year prices jump at renewal, so always read the renewal rate.

2. Hosting

Hosting is where your site's files are stored and served from. The range is wide:

  • Free static hosting — GitHub Pages, Netlify and Cloudflare Pages all have free tiers that are genuinely enough for a small brochure site. The catch is that they suit static pages (HTML/CSS), not a site that needs a database or a built-in content editor.
  • Paid shared hosting — typically a few dollars to around $15 per month, often with a free domain for the first year. Check renewal pricing and what happens when the introductory period ends.
  • Website builders — platforms like Wix, Squarespace and similar tools charge a monthly subscription. The advertised price is usually for an annual plan, and it often does not include a custom domain or professional email. Prices change often, so check the provider's current terms before budgeting.

3. Building it

  • Do it yourself — $0 in software if you use a template and free hosting, plus your time. A good template pack covers the design and structure; you supply the text and photos.
  • Done-for-you refresh — a fixed flat price for a defined scope, such as a five-page site rebuilt from your current content, published on hosting you control. Our website refresh is $750 with a 50% deposit and the balance after you approve the result.
  • Custom agency work — usually quoted per project and often in the thousands. You are paying for strategy, design, copywriting and project management, not just the pages.

The costs people forget

A website is not a one-time purchase. Budget for these recurring items:

ItemTypical costNotes
Domain renewal$10–$20 / yearSet auto-renew so you never lose the name.
Hosting$0–$180 / yearFree static hosting is $0; paid hosts vary.
Professional email$0–$100 / yearSome hosts include it free; otherwise it is a separate subscription.
Maintenance and updates$0–$300 / yearYour time if you do it yourself; a small retainer if someone else does.
Content and photos$0–$500+Your own photos are free; professional photography is optional.

The line that surprises people is maintenance. Software gets updated, plugins get patched, photos get swapped, and content goes stale. A site that is not maintained slowly becomes a security risk and a poor first impression.

Comparing your options honestly

There is no universally "right" choice — only the one that fits your time, budget and how much you want to handle yourself.

  • If you have time and want the lowest cost: a template pack plus free hosting gets you a real, fast site for about $49 one-time plus a domain. You do the editing and uploading.
  • If you want it handled for you: a fixed-price refresh with a clear scope, a deposit and a defined approval point. Ask exactly what is included before you commit.
  • If you want to edit it yourself forever: a website builder is convenient, but you are renting the platform, and moving off it later is harder than it looks.

Questions to ask before you hire anyone

These five questions will save you from most bad experiences:

  1. Who owns the domain and the hosting account? It should be you, in your name, with your email on the registration.
  2. Exactly what is included? Get the page count, the number of revision rounds, who writes the copy, and who supplies the photos in writing.
  3. What is the payment schedule? A deposit to start and a balance on approval is standard. Be cautious about paying 100% up front.
  4. What happens after launch? Is there a warranty period, who fixes problems, and what does ongoing maintenance cost?
  5. Can I move the site later? If the relationship ends, can you export your content and take it elsewhere?

Anyone who answers those clearly is probably fine. Anyone who dodges them is telling you something.

Quick checklist

  • Separate the three buckets: domain, hosting, build.

  • Budget for maintenance, not just launch.

  • Get scope, revisions and payment schedule in writing.
