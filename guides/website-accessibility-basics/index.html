Website Accessibility Basics: Make Your Site Usable for Everyone

Published 2026-11-08 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Accessibility means making your website usable by as many people as possible, including people who use screen readers, keyboards instead of a mouse, or larger text. It is good for customers, it reduces legal risk, and many of the fixes also help your search rankings. This guide covers the problems that show up most on small business sites and the fixes that do not require a rebuild.

What accessibility means in practice

Accessible sites work for people with a range of needs: someone who cannot see the screen and listens to it with a screen reader, someone who cannot use a mouse and navigates with a keyboard, someone who needs larger text or higher contrast, and someone using a phone in bright sunlight. Most accessibility work is about making sure the site works without relying on any one sense or input method.

Why it matters for a local business

  • Customers. A site that is hard to read or use costs you calls. The fixes are usually small.
  • Legal risk. In the United States, websites can be subject to accessibility requirements under the Americans with Disabilities Act and state law, and legal demands over inaccessible sites are common. There is no single "compliant" badge; the practical target is the WCAG guidelines published by the W3C.
  • Search. Many accessibility practices (clear headings, descriptive link text, alt text, fast pages) also help search engines understand your site.

The problems that show up most

1. Low contrast text

Light grey text on a white background is the single most common problem. Body text should have a strong contrast with its background. As a rule of thumb, if you can read it easily on a phone in sunlight, most people can.

2. Images with no alternative text

Screen readers rely on the alt attribute to describe an image. Every meaningful image needs a short, accurate description. Decorative images should use an empty alt so they are skipped.

<img src="team.jpg" alt="Two technicians beside a branded van">
<img src="divider.png" alt="">

3. A site you cannot use without a mouse

Some visitors navigate only with a keyboard. Every link, button and form field must be reachable and usable with the Tab key, and the focus indicator (the outline around the selected element) must stay visible.

4. Forms without labels

A form field that only has placeholder text disappears as soon as someone types, and screen readers may not announce it. Every field needs a real label tied to it.

<label for="phone">Phone number</label>
<input id="phone" name="phone" type="tel">

5. Headings used only to make text big

Headings are a map of the page. If you use them for visual size rather than structure, screen reader users lose the map. Use one h1 per page, then h2 and h3 in order.

6. Links that say "click here"

Screen reader users often jump between links. A link that says "click here" tells them nothing. Say where the link goes: "read our guide to Google reviews".

7. Tiny tap targets and dense layouts

On mobile, buttons and links need enough space around them to be tapped accurately, and text needs to reflow rather than cut off when the user zooms.

How to check your own site

  • Tab through it. Unplug your mouse and try to reach everything with the Tab and Enter keys.
  • Zoom to 200%. Text should reflow, not disappear.
  • Run an automated check. Free tools such as the WAVE browser extension or the accessibility audit in Chrome DevTools (Lighthouse) will flag the most common issues, including missing alt text and contrast failures.
  • Test with a screen reader. VoiceOver is built into macOS and iOS, and NVDA is a free Windows option. Even five minutes of listening will show you how much your site depends on visuals.

Automated tools catch a minority of issues, so use them as a starting point rather than a clean bill of health.

What you can fix yourself vs when to get help

Most of the problems above are content and template fixes: better contrast in your stylesheet, alt text on images, labels on form fields, and proper heading structure. If your site is built on a template, the template may already be mostly accessible; the gaps are usually in the content you added.

If you are buying a site, ask the builder specifically about accessibility: keyboard navigation, contrast, alt text and heading structure. If you have received a legal letter about accessibility, treat it as a legal matter and get professional advice rather than trying to handle it alone.

Quick checklist

  • Body text has strong contrast with its background.
  • Every meaningful image has alt text; decorative images have empty alt.
  • The whole site works with a keyboard alone.
  • Every form field has a real label.
  • Headings are used in order, not for styling.
  • Links say where they go.
  • Tap targets are large enough on mobile.
