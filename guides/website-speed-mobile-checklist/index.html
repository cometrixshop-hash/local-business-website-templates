Website Speed Checklist: Make Your Site Fast on Phones

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Visitors decide in a few seconds whether to stay on a website, and a slow one loses them before the first sentence is read. Speed is also a ranking signal Google has used for years. The good news: on a small business site, the causes are almost always the same handful of things, and most of them can be fixed for free.

Why speed matters more than it used to

Most local business website traffic now comes from phones, often on cellular data, sometimes on a weak signal. A page that loads in two seconds on office Wi-Fi can crawl on a phone across town. Google measures real-world mobile performance and uses it in ranking, and visitors simply leave slow pages — every extra second of waiting costs calls and quote requests.

The three numbers Google tracks

Google's Core Web Vitals are the specific speed metrics it uses for ranking. They sound technical, but each one is easy to picture:

MetricWhat it measuresGood target
LCP (Largest Contentful Paint)How long the biggest thing on the screen — usually a photo or a headline — takes to appear.2.5 seconds or less
INP (Interaction to Next Paint)How quickly the page responds when someone taps a button or a link.200 milliseconds or less
CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift)How much the page jumps around as it loads — images pushing text down, for example.0.1 or less

You can measure all three yourself for free with PageSpeed Insights or the Lighthouse tool built into Chrome. Run the test on your phone's connection, not just your office broadband, because that is what most of your customers are using.

The usual causes on small business websites

  • Huge images. A photo straight off a phone or a camera can be five or ten times larger than it needs to be. This is the single most common cause of a slow site.
  • Page builders and plugins loading more code than the page needs. Every extra script adds work for the phone.
  • Third-party widgets — live chat, review feeds, maps on every page, embedded videos — that load even when they are not used.
  • No caching. The server rebuilds the same page for every visitor instead of serving a stored copy.
  • Cheap shared hosting that is slow even when your site itself is fine.

A free speed checklist

Work through these in order. The first two usually deliver most of the improvement.

1. Compress every image before it goes on the site

Resize photos to the largest size they are actually displayed at — usually no more than 1600 pixels wide for a full-width image, and much less for thumbnails. Then compress them. Free tools like Squoosh (a web app from Google) or the built-in export settings in most photo apps do this. Aim for images under about 200 KB each where possible.

2. Use a modern image format

WebP is supported by every current browser and is usually 25–35% smaller than JPEG at the same quality. AVIF is even smaller but less universally supported. If your site is built from templates or a simple CMS, exporting to WebP is a quick win.

3. Load images only when they are needed

Add loading="lazy" to images below the fold so the phone does not download the whole page at once. The exception is the main hero image at the top, which should load immediately.

4. Remove scripts you do not use

Check the contact page, the home page and the service pages for widgets, chat tools, tracking scripts and embedded videos you added once and forgot about. If a script is not doing real work, delete it.

5. Turn on caching and compression

On static hosts like GitHub Pages, Netlify or Cloudflare Pages this is handled for you. On a traditional host, look for "caching" and "Gzip/Brotli compression" in the control panel, or ask the host to enable them.

6. Test, then test again

Re-run PageSpeed Insights after each change. A fix that helps the score but breaks the layout is not a fix, so also look at the page on a real phone afterwards.

What "good enough" looks like

You do not need a perfect score. A simple, fast site with a few well-compressed photos will usually beat a beautiful, bloated one. Focus on the things visitors actually notice: the page appearing quickly, the text not jumping, and buttons responding the moment they are tapped. Those are the three things that turn a visitor into a phone call.
