How to Make a Website for a Plumbing Business (and Host It Free)

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Most people who need a plumber are dealing with a problem right now: a leaking water heater, a backed-up drain, no hot water before work. They grab a phone, search, look at two or three sites, and call the first one that looks trustworthy and easy to reach. A plumbing website doesn't have to be fancy to win that call. It has to load fast, say clearly what you fix and where, and make calling you take one tap.

This guide covers building that kind of site yourself, from planning the pages to putting it online on free hosting. You don't need a monthly website builder subscription. A small static site (plain HTML and CSS files) is enough for most one-truck and small-crew plumbing businesses.

Step 1: Decide what the site actually has to do

Before thinking about colors or logos, write down the three jobs your website has:

  1. Confirm you're the right plumber. The visitor wants to see that you handle their problem (emergency repairs, drain cleaning, water heaters, repiping, remodels) and that you serve their town.
  2. Build enough trust to call. A real business name, a license number if your state issues one, photos of real work, honest reviews, and clear hours do this better than any slogan.
  3. Make contact effortless. A visible phone number, a tap-to-call button on mobile, and a short form for non-urgent jobs.

Everything on the page should serve one of those three jobs. If a section doesn't, cut it.

Step 2: Plan a one-page layout that converts

For a small plumbing business, a single well-organized page usually beats a sprawling multi-page site. Here's a structure that works, top to bottom:

  • Header with phone number. Your business name, a one-line description ("Licensed plumbers serving Springfield and nearby towns") and the phone number as a clickable link.
  • Hero section. One headline that names the problem you solve, one sentence of support, and two buttons: "Call now" and "Request a quote."
  • Services. Six to eight short cards: emergency plumbing, drain cleaning, water heater repair and replacement, leak detection, fixture installation, sewer line work. Two sentences each, in plain language.
  • How it works. Three steps: call or send a request, get an upfront estimate, job done and cleaned up. This answers the unspoken question "what happens if I call?"
  • Service area. List the cities, neighborhoods or ZIP codes you cover. This helps visitors and helps search engines understand where you work.
  • Reviews. Three or four real reviews with first name and town. Never write fake ones.
  • FAQ. Do you charge a trip fee? Do you offer emergency service? Are you licensed and insured? What payment methods do you take?
  • Contact section. Phone, email, hours, and a short form.

If you later want to rank for individual services, you can add separate pages such as "Water heater repair in Springfield." Start with one strong page and grow from there.

Step 3: Write copy customers understand

Write like you talk to customers on the phone. "We clear clogged drains, usually the same day" is better than "comprehensive drainage solutions." A few rules that help:

  • Lead with the outcome the customer wants (hot water back, drain flowing) rather than your equipment.
  • Mention your town and service area naturally in the headline or intro. Don't repeat city names dozens of times; it reads badly and doesn't help.
  • Be specific about what you don't do. If you don't handle commercial work or septic systems, say so. It saves you unqualified calls.
  • Only promise what you deliver. If "24/7" really means "until 10 pm," write that.

Step 4: Make it work on phones first

Many plumbing searches happen on a phone, often in a stressful moment, and Google itself uses the mobile version of a site's content for indexing and ranking. Design for a small screen first:

  • Include <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width,initial-scale=1"> in the page head so the layout scales properly.
  • Use a sticky "Call" button that stays at the bottom of the screen on mobile.
  • Make the phone number a real link so it dials when tapped:
<a href="tel:+15555550123">(555) 555-0123</a>
  • Keep text at a readable size (16px or larger) and buttons big enough to hit with a thumb.
  • Compress your photos. A photo straight off a phone can be several megabytes; resized to 1600px wide and saved as a JPEG or WebP, it's usually a small fraction of that.

We cover the must-have mobile elements in more depth in What every HVAC website needs to get calls; the same checklist applies to plumbers.

Step 5: Build the site

You have three realistic options:

  1. Write it from scratch. If you're comfortable with HTML and CSS, a one-page site is a weekend project. Keep it to one HTML file, one CSS file and an images folder.
  2. Start from a template. A ready-made static template gives you the layout, mobile behavior and form markup, and you replace the text and photos. This is the fastest route for most owners.
  3. Use a hosted builder. Builders are easy but usually charge a monthly fee, and moving your site off them later can be awkward.

Whichever you choose, add the basics search engines look for: a unique <title> such as "Springfield Plumber | Drain Cleaning & Water Heaters | Acme Plumbing," a meta description that reads like a short ad, and descriptive alt text on photos.

You should also add LocalBusiness structured data. Schema.org has a dedicated Plumber type, which is a more specific kind of LocalBusiness. A minimal example:

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Plumber",
  "name": "Acme Plumbing",
  "telephone": "+1-555-555-0123",
  "url": "https://www.acmeplumbing.example/",
  "address": {
    "@type": "PostalAddress",
    "addressLocality": "Springfield",
    "addressRegion": "IL",
    "addressCountry": "US"
  },
  "areaServed": ["Springfield", "Chatham", "Rochester"],
  "openingHours": "Mo-Fr 07:00-18:00"
}
</script>

Make sure the name, phone and address in that block match your Google Business Profile exactly. Our local SEO checklist explains why that consistency matters.

Step 6: Add a contact form that actually reaches you

A static site has no server code, so a form needs a service to receive submissions and email them to you. The two simplest options are Formspree (works on any host) and Netlify Forms (works if you host on Netlify). Both take a few minutes to set up. Add a hidden "honeypot" field to cut down on spam. The full walkthrough is in How to add a contact form to a static site.

Keep the form short: name, phone, town or ZIP, a description of the problem, and preferred contact time. Every extra field loses some people. And put a line above it that says, "For emergencies, call us instead," with the number.

Step 7: Put it online with free hosting

Static sites can be hosted for free on several reputable platforms. The three most common are:

  • Netlify: you can drag and drop your site folder onto its deploy page and get a live URL, and its free plan includes custom domains with SSL.
  • Cloudflare Pages: also supports uploading a folder or zip straight from your computer.
  • GitHub Pages: free and reliable, but GitHub's own usage limits say it isn't intended as free hosting for running an online business or e-commerce site. Read that page and decide for yourself; for a business site, Netlify or Cloudflare Pages is the more comfortable fit.

Step-by-step instructions for all three are in How to host a small business website for free.

The one thing you should pay for is your own domain name, typically a modest yearly fee from a registrar. "acmeplumbing.com" on your truck and invoices looks far more established than a free subdomain, and you keep it if you ever change hosts.

Step 8: Make sure it shows as secure

All three hosts above provide free HTTPS certificates. Turn on HTTPS enforcement once your domain is connected. This matters more than ever: Chrome now asks users for permission before connecting to an HTTP site by default. A warning screen in front of your phone number is not a good first impression. More on this in Why a 'Not secure' warning costs you customers.

Step 9: Launch checklist

  • Tap the phone link on your own phone and confirm it dials the right number.
  • Submit the contact form and confirm the email arrives (check spam too).
  • Open the site on a phone over mobile data, not just Wi-Fi, and see how quickly it loads.
  • Check that the address bar shows a secure connection with no warnings.
  • Paste your page into Google's Rich Results Test to check your structured data for errors.
  • Add your website URL to your Google Business Profile.
  • Set up Google Search Console and submit your sitemap.

After launch: keep it current

A plumbing website isn't a one-time project, but it doesn't need much upkeep either. Add new job photos every few months, update hours before holidays, add new reviews as they come in, and update the service area if you expand. Small, regular updates keep the site accurate, and accuracy is what customers and search engines both reward.
