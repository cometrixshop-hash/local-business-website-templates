How to Get More Google Reviews: The Rules, and What Actually Works

Published 2026-11-07 · Cometrix Web Studio guides

Reviews are one of the first things a potential customer checks, and they are one of many signals Google uses to decide which local businesses to show. You cannot buy your way to a good reputation, and you should not try: Google removes incentivized, fake and selectively solicited reviews, and penalties can hit the whole listing. This guide covers what Google actually allows, and the boring, repeatable habits that reliably earn genuine reviews.

What Google prohibits

Google's Prohibited & restricted content policy is explicit. It does not allow:

  • Incentivized reviews. Offering money, discounts, gift cards, free work or "a chance to win" in exchange for a review, or for removing a negative one.
  • Fake reviews. Reviews from people who did not actually use your service, reviews written by employees or paid reviewers, and reviews posted from multiple accounts by one person.
  • Selective solicitation. Only asking happy customers for reviews, or discouraging negative reviews. Ask everyone the same way.
  • On-premises pressure. Requiring or pressuring customers to leave a rating while they are still on your premises, and telling them what to write.
  • Competitor sabotage. Posting negative content on a competitor's listing.

The broader Business Profile policy overview explains that content which breaks these rules can be rejected or removed, and that repeat problems can affect the profile itself. A short-lived boost from a handful of questionable reviews is not worth that risk.

What actually works

Most businesses that earn reviews consistently do four unglamorous things well.

1. Ask every customer, at the moment they're happiest

The best time to ask is right after a job is finished well and the customer has said thanks — not a week later, and not at the point of sale. A simple, repeatable ask beats a clever one:

Thanks for choosing us, [name]. If you have a minute, an honest Google review helps a lot. Here's the link: [review link]. If something wasn't right, reply to this email instead — I'd rather fix it.

Notice the last sentence. Inviting criticism privately is both honest and good business: it gives an unhappy customer a direct path to you instead of to Google.

2. Make it one tap

Every extra click loses people. Put the review link in the places your customers already are:

  • A text or email follow-up after the job, sent within a day
  • Your email signature
  • A QR code on invoices, receipts, work shirts, magnets and business cards
  • A link in your website footer or contact page

3. Get your review link

Signed in to your Business Profile on a computer, open Read reviews and choose Get more reviews (sometimes labeled Ask for reviews). Google shows a short link you can copy and share, plus a QR code. Test the link on your own phone before you print anything.

If you cannot sign in — for example you manage a client's profile — you can build a link from the business's Place ID using https://search.google.com/local/writereview?placeid=YOUR_PLACE_ID. Google does not officially document that URL, and behavior can vary, so treat it as a fallback and test it on more than one device before relying on it.

4. Respond to reviews

Responding is not required, but it is one of the cheapest trust signals you have. Thank happy reviewers briefly and specifically. For negative reviews, respond once, calmly, and take the detail offline:

Thanks for letting us know, [name]. That's not the experience we want. I've sent you an email so we can make it right — please check your inbox.

Never argue, never post customer details, and never respond differently to positive and negative reviewers in a way that looks like review-gating. If a review is fake or violates policy, use the "flag" option in your Business Profile rather than arguing in public.

What not to do (quick checklist)

  • Never offer a discount, gift or payment for a review.
  • Never write reviews for your own business, or have staff do it from their personal accounts.
  • Never ask only your happiest customers, and never tell unhappy ones to skip it.
  • Never pressure anyone to review while they are on-site.
  • Never review a competitor.
  • Never buy reviews from a "reputation" service — these are routinely removed.
  • Never copy reviews from another site or post the same text repeatedly.

How many reviews do you need?

There is no magic number, and anyone who quotes one is guessing. What matters is a steady, recent stream of genuine reviews that reflect how you actually work. A business with 20 honest reviews and a thoughtful owner response usually outperforms one with 200 unearned stars.
